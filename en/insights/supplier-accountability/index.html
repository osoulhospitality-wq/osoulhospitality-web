Two contractors, two invoices: who owns the result?

Contracts can look perfectly clear when read one at a time. One firm maintains the pumps, another cleans the tanks and a third repairs finishes. An evening complaint rarely arrives with a contract reference attached. A resident reports interrupted water service or reception finds a corridor still closed after a visit. Someone must own the outcome across these contracts until a person at the building can accept the service again.

Trace a real incident across the boundaries

A contract might cover inspection while another covers materials but excludes fitting. A small gap between the two can leave everyone waiting for an approval whose owner nobody can name. Instructions remain in separate messages and accounts receives a signature that proves a technician entered the building. Taken individually, those records tell the owner little about who checked service after the different workers departed.

Select a recent incident and reconstruct its journey with the operator. Identify who received it, assigned priority, allowed access, involved another trade and informed the user that service had resumed. Wherever the explanation relies entirely on the memory of one person, examine what the next shift would have known. Even a case in which every supplier complied with its contract can reveal a serious gap in operational ownership.

Describe contract boundaries through situations

Take a low-pressure complaint rather than the abstract label “pump maintenance.” Who investigates first? Does the rate include attendance outside normal coverage? Who opens the plant room? What happens if the specialist finds the fault downstream in an apartment? Put the agreed answers in an interface schedule that a duty employee can use. The signed version should sit with the contract, with an accessible copy for the site team.

Assign one coordinator to follow each outcome through acceptance. That person may coordinate several trades without having authority to approve additional spending. Distinguish coordination, purchase approval and inspection in the schedule. Record roles first and keep the current duty contact separately. This allows the approach to survive staff changes and makes clear who can appoint a substitute when the usual contractor cannot attend.

Tie the invoice to the instruction

Attendance, materials and additional work may arrive on one invoice. Require each item to identify the job and contract provision under which it was ordered. For extras, find the authorised approval and the change that made extra work necessary. Resolving a question while the supervisor still remembers the visit is easier than trying to explain aggregated spend after the reporting period has closed.

Fragmentation can show up as repeat diagnostic visits, a modest approval holding a space out of use or reception repeatedly arranging entry to an occupied unit. Capture time without an assigned owner separately from time waiting for a part or an access permission. The financial effect may be similar for the owner, but the corrective action belongs in very different parts of the next contract.

Accept attendance and completion separately

The visiting contractor records its observations, intervention and supporting evidence. A property representative then checks the agreed acceptance criterion. If a room is occupied or related equipment is unavailable, record that verification is outstanding and name the person arranging it. This permits acknowledgement that the contractor attended while reserving the decision on completion and payment according to the contract.

For a cross-trade dispute, begin a short review with the incident record and the instructions issued. Ask each supplier what it can substantiate and where its responsibility ended. Assign the next action and review date before the meeting closes. If the gap is likely to recur, change the interface schedule or reception instructions while the facts are accessible. Preserve any temporary spending approval for later reconciliation.

Illustrative field example

Imagine a damp ceiling above a corridor after a technical visit. A finishes contractor offers to reinstate the surface while an equipment specialist requests authority to investigate the nearby system. The contract lead places both jobs under one incident, arranges appropriate site controls and seeks competent diagnosis before commissioning reinstatement. Each phase now has its own deliverable, evidence and acceptance owner. Essential service arrangements can proceed under the site plan while commercial responsibility is resolved.

When the two invoices arrive, the owner checks that the first corresponds to the authorised diagnosis and intervention and that the second followed permission to reinstate. Similar invoice dates alone do not establish duplication. Different supplier names alone do not establish entitlement either. The decision turns on the issued scopes and accepted outcomes, including whether the corridor was handed back for use.

Questions for renewal

  • Ask the contractor to map a recent fault that crossed a trade boundary and identify the first escalation point.
  • Trace selected invoices to procurement authority, visit records and on-site acceptance by an authorised person.
  • Verify who may call an alternative supplier if the contracted one cannot attend and who initially bears the cost.
  • Check that the duty team can access the scope and reach a decision maker during the agreed coverage.
  • Compare the quality and recurrence of outcomes separately from the number of contracted visits delivered.
  • Agree how exclusions are valued before the next urgent incident makes the price difficult to challenge.

For unresolved cases, record which costs are provisional and who will decide final allocation when evidence arrives. An owner can retain several excellent specialists. The management task is to ensure that an interface between their contracts never becomes a place where responsibility for restoring the property quietly disappears.
