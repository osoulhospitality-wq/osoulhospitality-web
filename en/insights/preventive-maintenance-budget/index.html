Funding preventive work when repairs consume the budget

At month end, the owner has spent more on maintenance than expected, yet preventive work keeps moving back because the team is busy answering faults. Paying for more visits without understanding what happens on each visit may make the problem larger. Link funding to the risk the property carries, the specific task proposed and the evidence that the task was completed under the conditions that make it useful.

Find where the available hours go

Gather work orders due over the same period. Identify what was done, what moved and why. A room may be occupied, consumables may be missing or a contractor may be waiting for access. An order might show complete after a general attendance visit. Separate these situations to determine whether the constraint lies in staffing, procurement, access planning or the quality of instructions issued.

Talk to the duty employee who reallocates work when an urgent report arrives. Who chooses which task moves and who sets its new date? If outstanding work disappears when the next month’s plan is generated, completion can look healthy while the backlog grows. Retain the original due date, reason for change and the person who accepted the impact on availability, warranty or operations.

Consider the operating consequences of a failure

Bring the operator, technical specialist and reservations representative together when assessing consequences. The most expensive machine is not automatically the top priority. Ask which areas rely on it, whether an alternative exists, how long parts take to obtain and what manufacturer instructions and applicable requirements demand. Consider these together when scarce access windows cannot accommodate every task at once.

Seasonal occupancy may change the best time to do the work even when its technical content remains constant. Equipment may be easier to release when spare rooms are available than during a group arrival. Show the owner the feasible windows and their impact. Where an approved task needs to move, the competent person records the technical basis and necessary authorisation, including any constraint that prevents deferral.

Commission a task that can be accepted

A document headed “periodic maintenance” provides little basis for inspection. Specify the asset, task, governing reference, result to be recorded and limits of the worker’s authority. The assignment may require specialist inspection, measurement, replacement of a consumable or investigation of a previous alert. Agree what fits the actual equipment and how the supervisor will establish that the report belongs to it.

When a preventive visit finds a defect, create a linked corrective instruction with its service impact and follow-up priority. The original inspection can be accepted without allowing the finding to disappear. The owner then sees whether paid preventive work actually took place and whether the result raised a further funding decision. Preserve the due date of that corrective work independently of the inspection schedule.

Present the budget as an explicit choice

What does existing expenditure cover? Labour only, or also parts, consumables, access and independent inspection? Do callouts repeatedly concern one asset? Is stock held locally and what supports the supplier’s lead-time estimate? Base any downtime estimate on operating evidence and disclose assumptions affecting the comparison. The owner can then test whether a change in access or parts availability would alter the recommendation.

For a new preventive item, describe what will change in the schedule, who will accept the result and how its value will be reviewed. Do not promise a saving before a baseline and comparable period exist. A task can be justified by service continuity or an applicable requirement even if its financial effect cannot be separated from changing occupancy or concurrent capital replacement.

Illustrative field example

Consider an aparthotel operator asking to increase visit frequency because faults are recurring. Reviewing instructions shows that contractors frequently arrived when apartments could not be released and marked jobs complete on attendance. Before buying extra coverage, the manager coordinates workable windows with reception, adds a separate status for denied access and agrees rebooking and charging rules. The decision changes the quality of each visit before changing its price.

At review the team compares tasks actually completed with tasks due, then examines faults on the relevant equipment. If delivery improves but one machine keeps failing, diagnosis or replacement deserves attention. If missing consumables remain the constraint, change procurement before purchasing more labour. Neither conclusion would be visible from a chart that treated every visit as a completed preventive task.

Funding checklist

  • Can the team explain the source of each task interval, the authority for changes and the last actual completion?
  • Do priorities reflect service impact, alternatives and supply delays, with technical and operator input?
  • Are deferred dates and reasons retained when a new monthly plan is issued?
  • Do cost comparisons include materials, labour, downtime and occupancy coordination on a consistent basis?
  • Can a preventive finding be traced to corrective action and an explicit decision on funding?
  • Have the owner and operator agreed the measure, its data source and the reasons that would justify changing course?

End the review with a decision the site can implement: change an access window, approve a part, revise specialist scope or provide a continuity arrangement. Keep the reasoning with the plan, including tasks retired after equipment replacement. The next supervisor should know why money was committed and which evidence will allow the owner to reassess that commitment.
